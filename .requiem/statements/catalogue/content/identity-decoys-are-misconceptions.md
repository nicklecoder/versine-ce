An identities choice question's wrong options are slips a student makes, never random filler (sin 6θ offered for 1 − 2 sin²θ teaches nothing, because no one would pick it). A simplify question crosses two slips (see decoys-cross-two-slips): the answer, each slip alone, and both together, taken from the partner function (sin²θ for cos²θ, cot θ for tan θ, csc θ for sec θ), the reciprocal, a square dropped or added, a sign flipped (the one in the middle of a sum when there is one, 1 + 2 sin²θ for 1 − 2 sin²θ), or one of the misconceptions listed per template (2 sin θ for sin 2θ, sin 4θ for 4 sin θ cos θ) with the other slip applied to it too: sin 2θ against 2 sin θ, cos 2θ and 2 cos θ. Crossed, each option is one slip from two others, so the answer is not the option nearest all the rest. Only when no pair of slips gives four distinct options does the old order apply: the listed misconceptions, then the answer's negative, then the general pool. A proof's next line crosses two slips in that line, or, when a line already written is offered, crosses that step back with one slip made in both. The exactly-one-equal guarantee is unchanged: a slip that happens to equal the answer is skipped.
